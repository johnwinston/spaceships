Welcome aboard

Every card holds a tiny spaceship

Each one lives in Conway's Game of Life, a "game" with no players that John Conway invented in 1970. You set up a few live cells on a grid, then watch: every generation, each cell lives or dies by counting its neighbours.

Scroll on for how it works, where spaceships come from, and how people still find new ones today.

Meet Conway

How he invented it

Conway tells the story himself. Martin Gardner published the game in Scientific American in October 1970, and people have been finding new things in it ever since.

The rules

Count the neighbours

3 neighbours: a cell is born. 2 or 3: it stays. Anything else: it's gone.

Every cell updates at the same moment. That's the whole game, written B3/S23.

Your turn

Draw something and press play

Tap cells to draw, or load a pattern. Most shapes fizzle out. A few keep going.

R-pentomino

The first natural glider

Five cells that refuse to settle down. At generation 69, Richard Guy spotted a little piece break off and walk away: the first glider anyone had seen. Nobody designed it.

Spaceships

Patterns that fly

A spaceship comes back to its own shape, one step over, and keeps going forever. Conway found these three in 1970. The glider is the smallest of all.

No formula

Every ship is searched for

No equation spits out spaceships. The only way to know what a pattern does is to run it, and there are far too many to try. So searches build a ship one row at a time and throw away rows that can't work.

Half a ship

A partial is a ship whose front works but whose back isn't finished, so it falls apart. Here's Sir Robin, the first knight-move ship, cut short next to the whole thing. ikpx2 grows partials until one closes up.
