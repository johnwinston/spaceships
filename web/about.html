Where Life came from

John von Neumann 1903–1957

In the 1940s he asked whether a machine could build a copy of itself. With Stanislaw Ulam he pictured a grid of cells, each one changing by looking only at its neighbours, and designed a pattern on it that could copy itself. It worked on paper, but every cell needed 29 different states.

John Conway 1937–2020

In 1970 he asked how simple a world like that could be and still surprise you. He cut it down to two states, alive or empty, and a few rules about neighbours. He called it Life, and it turned out to hold gliders, spaceships and even machines that copy themselves.

How Conway found it

Conway tells the story himself. Martin Gardner published Life in Scientific American in October 1970, and people have been finding new things in it ever since.

Count the neighbours

Every square is alive or empty. Each generation, every square counts how many of its eight neighbours are alive, and that number decides what happens next.

born dies the square being counted. People write the whole rule as B3/S23.

Draw something and press play

Tap squares to draw, or load a pattern. Most shapes fizzle out. A few keep going.

The first spaceship

Five squares that refuse to settle down. At generation 69, Richard Guy spotted a small piece break off and walk away. It was a glider, the first spaceship anyone had seen, and nobody designed it.

Every spaceship is different

A spaceship comes back to its own shape a few steps over and keeps going forever. Like pieces in a generative art collection, each one has its own traits: how fast it moves, how often it repeats, how big it is. Each card follows its ship, so the grid slides past at the ship's real speed. Watch which one moves fastest.

Every ship is searched for

No formula produces spaceships. The only way to know what a pattern does is to run it, and there are far too many to try. So searches build a ship one row at a time and throw away rows that can't work.

Half a ship

A partial is a ship whose front works but whose back isn't finished, so it falls apart. I gave ikpx2 the front of Sir Robin, the first ship that moves like a chess knight, and let it search for 20 minutes.

The Life Pattern collection

Catagolue keeps a separate NFT collection of famous Life patterns, each one notable enough for its own LifeWiki page. A pattern gets in by being at least one of these:

  • Historic
  • Very well known
  • Answered an open question
  • Set a record
  • Did something first
  • Made the news

Doo-dah, the 2c/7 ship I found in December 2020, is number 1. It has its own LifeWiki page and made The New York Times.
