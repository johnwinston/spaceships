Origin of Life?

John von Neumann 1903–1957

  • Asked if a machine could build a copy of itself.
  • Drew a grid of cells that only look at their neighbours.
  • His copying machine needed 29 states per cell.

John Conway 1937–2020

  • Asked how simple that world could be.
  • Cut it to two states: alive or dead.
  • Called it Life, in 1970.

Count the neighbours

Every square is alive or dead. Each generation, every square counts how many of its eight neighbours are alive, and that number decides what happens next.

Draw something and press play

Tap squares to draw, or load a pattern. Most shapes fizzle out. A few keep going.

The first spaceship

Five squares that refuse to settle down. At generation 69, Richard Guy spotted a small piece break off and walk away. It was a glider, the first spaceship anyone had seen, and nobody designed it.

A little creature, emerging from the chaos.

Every spaceship is different

  • A spaceship is a tiny creature that walks forever.
  • Nobody draws them. They emerge from the randomness.
  • Each has its own gait, size and speed.

Same squares, new rules

Change the counting and everything changes. These are the same five squares under Conway's rule, then under two rules from my collection. One settles, and one grows a never-ending snowflake.

99 ships, 99 rules

I wrote a search that makes up random rules and looks for spaceships in each one. Every ship on these cards lives in a universe of its own.

Every ship is searched for

No formula produces spaceships. The only way to know what a pattern does is to run it, and there are far too many to try. So searches build a ship one row at a time and throw away rows that can't work.

It works like mining crypto. Computers grind through billions of guesses, and every so often one strikes a ship.

The search software is my paintbrush. I don't draw the creature. I coax it out of the noise.

Join the search
