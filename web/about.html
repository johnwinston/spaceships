Every card holds a tiny spaceship

Each one lives in its own rule, a cousin of Conway's Game of Life, the game with no players that John Conway found in 1970. Scroll down to see how Life works and where the ships come from.

How Conway found it

Conway tells the story himself. Martin Gardner published the game in Scientific American in October 1970, and people have been finding new things in it ever since.

Count the neighbours

Each square on the grid is alive or empty. Every generation, it counts its eight neighbours.

An empty square with exactly 3 live neighbours is born. A live square with 2 or 3 survives. With fewer than 2 it dies of loneliness, and with more than 3 it dies of overcrowding.

In the animation, gold squares are born and red ones die. That's the whole game. People write it as B3/S23.

Draw something and press play

Tap squares to draw, or load a pattern. Most shapes fizzle out. A few keep going.

The first spaceship

Five squares that refuse to settle down. At generation 69, Richard Guy spotted a small piece break off and walk away. It was a glider, the first spaceship anyone had seen, and nobody designed it.

Patterns that fly

A spaceship comes back to its own shape one step over, and keeps going forever. The glider is the smallest one. Conway found these three bigger ones in 1970 too.

Same squares, new rules

Change the counting and everything changes. These are the same five squares under Conway's rule, then under two rules from my collection. One settles, and one grows a never-ending snowflake.

Every ship is searched for

No formula produces spaceships. The only way to know what a pattern does is to run it, and there are far too many to try. So searches build a ship one row at a time and throw away rows that can't work.

Half a ship

A partial is a ship whose front works but whose back isn't finished, so it falls apart. I gave ikpx2 the front of Sir Robin, the first ship that moves like a chess knight, and let it search for 20 minutes.

It found 722 partials. Here are five of them, with the rows ikpx2 added in gold. None closed up, so each one crumbles.

What makes a pattern famous

Most patterns never get their own LifeWiki page. One earns a page by being at least one of these:

  • Historic
  • Very well known
  • Answered an open question
  • Set a record
  • Did something first
  • Made the news
